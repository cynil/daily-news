Aesop’s tortoise would have felt at home in Brussels. Slow-moving, deliberative and apparently untroubled by the spectacle of nimbler creatures racing ahead, the plodding reptile shares many qualities with the European Union. The ancient fable has the hare dashing off showily before losing interest in the race. Ukraine’s struggle against Russian revanchism has offered a modern retelling. America originally sprinted ahead as Europe dithered; politicians in Washington were on hand to deliver missiles to Ukraine even as those in Berlin were offering to send helmets.

Yet for all its military prowess America’s enthusiasm has proved erratic, to put it mildly. Europe, by contrast, has spent four years inching forward. Yes, it has squabbled over many decisions and sometimes seemed incapable of getting out of its own way. But in its own unflashy manner the European tortoise has kept moving, powered by a determination seemingly greater than its public disagreements. What it lacks in agility, the EU has made up for in staying power.

The remarkable thing is not how slowly Europe has moved, but that it has kept moving at all. The continent’s endurance in supporting Ukraine has surprised even its supporters. For years suspicions have abounded that “war fatigue” was about to sap the continent’s resolve. (Ukrainians, plenty fatigued, do not alas have the luxury of deciding they have had enough.) And yet look who keeps showing up to help, even as America has at times seemed to put more pressure on Ukraine than Russia. Just this week the process of unlocking a further €45bn ($49bn) in EU financial support to Ukraine has begun, no matter the strained public finances of many member states. The bloc is embarking on a 22nd round of sanctions, undeterred by the fractious haggling that was a feature of the 21 previous rounds. Talks with Ukraine about joining the EU—or at least closely integrating it into the club pending full membership further down the line—are ongoing. Nearly five years into the fighting, millions of Ukrainians enjoy refugee-like protection (and generous welfare provisions) wherever in Europe they might choose to settle. Fatigued, moi?

Granted, EU decision-making about Ukraine sometimes makes tortoises look dynamic. The most recent squabbling has concerned who might represent Europe at peace talks between Russia and Ukraine. For years now the continent has been asked to come up with preferably a single person to represent its interests at the negotiating table. Jared Kushner, the presidential son-in-law who serves as one of America’s envoys, recently quipped that getting Europe to agree on just one interlocutor was harder than getting Russia to accept having them at the negotiating table at all. As if to prove his point, at a summit on November 13th the Europeans put forward a team of no fewer than seven representatives to speak for them—one each from Germany, France, Britain, Italy, Poland, the EU institutions and NATO. (No Luxembourg?) It may seem absurd to have more Europeans at the table than every other party combined. But the truly mad feature of the talks is that America is sitting in the chair of mediator, seemingly neutral between the Ukrainian victim and the Russian aggressor. For its part, Europe is unambiguously on the side of Ukraine.

Unity of purpose is perhaps unsurprising among EU countries fearful that they might be next on President Vladimir Putin’s menu if Ukraine loses (and many of which already face nebulous “hybrid attacks” steered from the Kremlin). Some disagreements have been unedifying. Greece recently wangled an exemption from EU sanctions so its shipping fleet could keep ferrying Russian gas. Belgium has stood in the way of Europe seizing frozen Russian assets that many would like to see handed over to Ukraine. Promising Ukraine will join the EU is laudable, and could one day be a large carrot to get Ukrainians to accept a painful peace involving territorial losses to Russia. Yet in Brussels diplomatic circles the prospect is thought to be at least a decade away. If that might justifiably come as a disappointment to Ukrainians, who once hoped to join by 2027, they could be reminded that America suddenly eased sanctions on Russia ahead of midterm elections earlier this month. Better a plodding friend than a fickle one.

Slow-and-steady as the Europeans have been, there is always a risk that their resolve will reach its limits. Notably, a slew of forthcoming elections could shift the calculus of solidarity. At least three of the five European countries in the negotiating group will go to the polls in 2027. Populists in Italy and Poland are pro-Ukraine, but might need support from Kyiv-bashing allies to form coalitions. The biggest risk is the prospect of Marine Le Pen winning the French presidency. Although her tone on Ukraine has softened—and her xenophobic National Rally party is no longer financed by a Russian bank—she says France cannot afford to send more money to the cause. Somewhat in anticipation, European leaders are seeing if they might hurriedly sign off on a long-term EU budget that includes an additional €100bn in funding for Ukraine, locking in support before the political winds change.

Europe’s plodding yet purposeful approach has worked well enough so long as the job has been merely to keep Ukraine supplied with money, weapons and prospects for the future. Peace negotiations may demand something different. Alas, for now substantive talks seem a distant prospect. Russia continues to issue impossible demands as a precondition for bringing its war of aggression to an end, including NATO troops being pulled out of central Europe. Mr Putin seems to be betting that another winter of fighting will wear down Ukrainian resistance. Should a deal come within reach, Europe may need to move faster than it is used to. Until then, the tortoise has little reason to envy the hare.■
