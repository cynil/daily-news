# My War Against Leaf Blowers and Other Noise Crimes

- 作者：Sarah Miller
- 发布时间：2026-11-26
- 来源：The New Yorker
- 原文链接：<https://www.newyorker.com/culture/personal-history/my-lonely-war-against-noise>
- 字数：2787（正文段落 33）

I live between two small business districts in a quaint little California mountain village. Sunlight streams through enormous cedars. The speed limit is a lazy twenty-five miles per hour, the poppies in the drought-resistant gardens cartoonishly bright. But if you are feeling jealous maybe you shouldn’t, because a large part of each day is ruined for me by the sound of leaf blowers. Most households near me have at least one. They are such cherished objects that I imagine them tucked up in garages and sheds inside satin-lined cribs. You might assume that tidying one’s lawn once a week would suffice, but many people seem to prefer to do it daily. There is an apartment complex across the street, and a condo complex just next to it, and, if their maintenance crews are both working at the same time of day, there might be as many as four blowers going at once, a sadistic twist on a barbershop quartet.

A few years ago, I tried to gauge my neighbors’ interest in trying to ban or greatly restrict the use of gas-powered leaf blowers. It was obvious that I was outnumbered; my frayed sanity was all the proof I needed that the neighborhood’s pro-blower contingent was strong. Still, I figured at least one or two people might share my rage that no stretch of quiet—not at dawn, not at sunset, not on Sundays—was too sacred to be blasted to smithereens in the name of landscaping.

You’d think I’d said that I wanted to get rid of pets, or running water. “How will I blow out my gutters?” one neighbor asked, his lower lip quivering. I’d seen him blow out the gutters on his roof only once. His lawn, meanwhile, looked like it belonged on a Netflix show where someone with a brand new Audi has just been murdered. The neighbors who did not use the devices seemed unbothered by the people who did. One day, I was standing in the middle of the street trying to figure out the whereabouts of what sounded like seven different leaf blowers. One of my neighbors got out of her car and, seeing my distress, said, “Aww, poor thing,” as if I were a baby who had just dropped my binky rather than one of many adult Americans being driven mad by machines that have, by the way, been banned or restricted in some two hundred cities and towns across the U.S. (These bans and restrictions are specifically on gas-powered leaf blowers; electric ones are considerably quieter, less carcinogenic, and less harmful to the environment, though I can’t say I love them either.)

According to the World Health Organization, “excessive” noise “seriously harms human health and interferes with people’s daily activities at school, at work, at home and during leisure time.” Noise stresses the heart and the nervous system. According to the European Environmental Agency, an estimated twenty-two million people suffer from noise-related “chronic high annoyance.” If I lived in Europe, that figure would be twenty-two million and one. In the U.S., an estimated sixty-three hundred annual deaths are caused by heart disease linked to road, rail, and aircraft noises. A newer noise-pollution horror is A.I. data centers, which emit a single-frequency, persistently annoying hum as they consume ungodly quantities of water and electricity. Livestock farmers have complained that the sound is causing their animals to lose weight and stop reproducing. Recently, residents of Carroll County, Kentucky, recorded the sound of a nearby data center and played it for county officials at a public meeting, in order to give them “a dose of what they’re asking us to endure.”

You may by now have suspected that I hate noise more than your average person, perhaps even more than your average noise-hating person. My mother tells me that I was a bizarrely uncomplaining baby, but that I’d begin to sob the moment a vacuum cleaner or a lawnmower started up. I even hated fans. A few years ago, while watching the movie “Tár,” I was gratified to learn, via the protagonist, played by Cate Blanchett, that Arthur Schopenhauer viewed sensitivity to noise as a sign of brilliance. In Schopenhauer’s 1851 essay “On Noise,” he writes that “the most eminent intellects have always been strongly averse to any kind of disturbance, interruption and distraction, and above everything to that violent interruption which is caused by noise; other people do not take any special notice of this sort of thing.”

I would end up watching “Tár” repeatedly, the way some people watch “Bridget Jones’s Diary” or “Dirty Dancing.” But, even before it came out, I suspected that there was something about hating leaf blowers that was élitist or self-regarding. If a gas-powered two-stroke engine was the pea, I did not wish to be cast in the role of the princess, especially Princess Karen.

So I abandoned my mission, but not without resentments, and with the passage of time my neighborhood has only gotten louder. The 2018 fire in Paradise, California, about fifty miles from my town of Nevada City, was ruled to have been caused in part by the Pacific Gas & Electric Company failing to remove vegetation near transmission lines, and as restitution the company has been financing aggressive preventative tree trimming. For the past few months, the city has been building a new sidewalk, adding cement mixers, jackhammers, stonecutters, idling trucks, and more to the base-level cacophony, sometimes starting as early as six-thirty in the morning. My neighbor has been doing construction on his house, much of it involving his own stonecutter, which he consorts with on weekend mornings.

There is also the matter of the Trump house.

My neighborhood is solidly blue. The one house with a Trump sign in front of it is known, naturally, as the Trump house. With repeated use, much of the vitriol has drained from this label, but it remains useful when answering questions such as, *Where the fuck is that noise coming from?* A loudly buzzing porch light. A Weedwacker going all day. The Shop-Vac that is best friends with the Weedwacker. The Trump house is not the main vector of neighborhood noise pollution (that distinction goes to the condo complex), but it is certainly where one finds the most varied examples of it, at the most startling times of day.

The buzzing outdoor light made its début during the early, life-is-absolutely-over days of *COVID*, and came on every evening, just as I was preparing to sit on my front porch and get wasted. After about two weeks of enduring the buzz, I went up to the Trump house and knocked on the door. A woman answered, and I told her that her outside light was so loud that I could hear it two blocks away. Could she please, please help me? She was apologetic and unscrewed the bulb right away, promising to find a less disruptive one. I have never gone over there again to complain about other noises, because I want to preserve the memory of that positive experience, and because the house is such a constant source of domestic-noise innovation that I wouldn’t even know where to begin.

On a recent Wednesday, I was in and out of my house, doing errands. During one of my short stints at home, I thought that I heard a beeping noise. I blamed the sidewalk. But that night, as I sat down on the couch to read a book called “Lineages of Revolt,” there it was. The sidewalk crew was gone, and the noise remained. It sounded like a truck backing up, but intermittently: three beeps, a significant pause, then three beeps again. There was just enough time between each series for me to imagine that the noise had gone away. But it always came back, so that I was caught in an endless loop of hope, betrayal, hope, betrayal.

I texted my neighbor Robin to ask if she was hearing the noise. Yes, she replied, it had been going all day, and it was driving her nuts.

Now, Robin is a good friend of mine, but I have to admit that when she said this I felt indignant. Why hadn’t she done anything about it? Why must I be the one to do something about everything? I got that a lot of people probably thought this about themselves, and we couldn’t all be right. Still, I reviewed some important facts: I’d been the only girl sent to the principal’s office for telling our Spanish teacher to stop making comments about our bodies. At the law firm I worked at briefly after college, I’d been the only one to complain about unpaid overtime. I found myself out of examples, still, it remained obvious to me that I was a lone courageous voice for the common good. So when Robin texted me again about the noise, I told her that I would take care of it.

We agreed that the beeps were probably coming from the Trump house. But they weren’t. I stood stock-still in the road for about seven minutes, listening to four series of beeps, in order to determine this. The sound was not on our street. Our row of houses abuts a vacant lot, and I sensed that the source was on the other side.

I went home, put on a bra, and ventured out again, ruminating on the various indignities of our present condition: gas, seven dollars a gallon; blueberries, eleven dollars a pint; and a person can’t even end the day peacefully reading a book about how the capitalist mode of production had destroyed the promise of the Arab Spring without listening to some terrible noise. I walked around to the road parallel to my road. But the beeping was getting farther away. I felt a familiar fear: this noise was never going to go away.

I had never called the police in my life. When, years ago, my drunk neighbor drove his giant truck into my car and my ex’s car, totalling both in one fell swoop, we did not call the police, because we didn’t want the guy to get arrested or go to jail. I was an adamant non police caller, and yet now I found myself dialling non-emergency services, knowing full well that that’s who would be dispatched. A woman answered, and I said, “Hi, good evening, just so you know, this isn’t an emergency,” because I wanted to signal that I was a reasonable person who knew the difference between a noise and a life-or-death situation.

She said, “Go ahead.” She might as well have said, “Tell Mama everything.” I unburdened myself, withholding no detail about the noise and its effects on my emotional and psychological health. She asked if I had any idea what it was.

“I don’t,” I said. “Maybe someone left the door of a brand-new refrigerator open and went out of town and then died?”

I texted Robin and told her that help was on the way. She said, “Good.”

Two cops showed up. One was civil, with attentive posture. The other was a silent lurker. If I had to draw the first cop, I would draw a brick wearing a hat. For the second cop, I would just draw a stick figure with a rectangle at the end of one arm: his phone. I never once saw his eyes or even the front of him.

I explained that the noise was recurring every minute or so. “I’m not crazy,” I said, knowing that the second you say you’re not crazy, people just think “crazy.” I said, “Just listen. Don’t move. Give it two minutes.”

We all stood there in silence in the middle of the street. Eventually, we heard it: beep, beep, beep. “All right,” said the Brick, pointing. “It’s coming from that way.” The Stick nodded. “You said there’s an empty lot back there?” the Brick asked, walking toward it.

“Yes,” I said, certain that the sound was not coming from the empty lot. *Idiots*, I thought, and headed off by myself to check the street I’d given up on before.

When Schopenhauer wrote “On Noise,” he was living in Frankfurt and fed up with carriage drivers’ incessant cracking of whips, which he believed only made their horses more apt to misbehave. The sound, he wrote, “must be regarded as something purely wanton; nay, as an impudent defiance.” I, like Schopenhauer, simply hated useless noises, I told myself. The din of a city—honking, shouting, sirens—I explicitly enjoyed. I had never once complained about the long and excruciating sidewalk project, because I was aware that technology had not yet produced a silent sidewalk-building machine. I applauded the city’s efforts to bring our beautiful sidewalk earsplittingly into being! But these beeps were purely wanton; nay, an impudent defiance.

The police came toward me, and the Brick said, “I think I found it.” They led me to a long and, in my opinion, quite hidden driveway on the street next to mine. I had to give them credit for finding in ten minutes a house that I hadn’t noticed after living in the neighborhood for ten years.

It was a very steep driveway. I waited at the bottom while the cops took their sweet time walking up to the house. If they knocked on the door and no one was home, I was going to run up there and break a window and find whatever was making the noise. But then I heard a voice, followed by smashing sounds, and then the cops were walking down the driveway.

“Well?” I asked.

“It’s just a guy with a broken CO<sub>2</sub> monitor,” the Brick said. The Stick went to get the car. “It was going off, so he put it outside.”

The beeping persisted. I pointed this out.

“He’s going to take care of it,” the Brick said.

I wasn’t at all convinced that someone who had left a loudly beeping sensor outside his house, tormenting his neighbors, could be relied on to take care of anything.

“Can’t you just wait until the thing is actually off?” I asked. Then I added, apologetically, “It’s just that I have been losing my mind a bit.”

“I understand,” the Brick said. “This is a quiet neighborhood.” But then he got in the cruiser with the Stick, and they were gone.

“It’s not a quiet neighborhood!” I shouted after the car. “It’s nothing but noise, all day and night!”

Up at the house, there were more smashes, then a few beeps from the offending object, then one final, feeble bleat. I went home, went to bed, and awoke in the morning to the sound of jackhammers pounding the asphalt—at least a useful noise. The leaf blowers started up at around nine. I Googled “Why are leaf blowers so awful.” I read that they produce both a low-frequency rumble from the engine and a high-pitched whine from the moving blades. This was an “Aha!” moment for me. There were two conspirators: bad on their own, evil when combined.

Robin came by and thanked me for stopping the noise. I said that I thought maybe leaf blowers were literally making me insane. She said that she would go to the city council with me to start advocating for a ban. I couldn’t believe it. I had an ally at last. “What about Kurt?” I asked. I was under the impression that her boyfriend, Kurt, loved his leaf blower. But apparently he’d put it in a pile of stuff to sell. Maybe I would buy it and bury it.

Later that week, I talked to Bonnie Sager, an optometrist who lives in Huntington, Long Island, and has spent fourteen years fighting gas-powered leaf blowers. She even managed to get her local town board to restrict them, but then landscapers in the area teamed up and got the policy suspended. Then they defeated a more modest version of the measure, and with it ancillary protections that had been proposed, such as no blowing on Sundays, or after 6 *P.M.* “You can’t get away from them,” Sager said. “They’re at the library. They’re at the bank. They’re at the supermarket. You come home and there they are again. I’m listening to leaf blowers right now.” I said that I was, too. Sager said that even though she had never been able to get rid of leaf blowers in her town, she’d had the vicarious enjoyment of seeing their use curbed in many other places: Portland, Oregon; San Francisco; Washington, D.C. She added, cheerfully, that she’d met some of her best friends in the leaf-blower-fighting community. I thanked her for speaking to me and added, before hanging up, “And for showing me my future.” ♦
