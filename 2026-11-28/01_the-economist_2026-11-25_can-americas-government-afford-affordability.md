# Can America’s government afford “affordability”?

*The Economist · 2026-11-25*

ONE UNSEXY word became the Democrats’ winning battle cry in America’s midterm elections in early November: “affordability”. Donald Trump has not got the message. On November 25th, as fresh data showed annual inflation climbing to 3.8%, his administration proposed another round of tariffs on imports from 14 trading partners. China was threatened with duties of up to 20%, on top of existing levies; Vietnam with as much as 35%. A tax on imports is a peculiar answer to voters’ complaints about living costs.

Democrats have a different answer to America’s affordability woes: open the public purse. Fresh from their midterm triumph, they are promising tax relief, more generous benefits and a helping hand with seemingly every household expense. Yet lavishing more public money on an already booming economy is a peculiar answer, too. GDP surged at a blistering annualised rate of 4.3% in the third quarter, the fastest in three years. Uncle Sam, meanwhile, ran a deficit of 6.2% of GDP last fiscal year, the sort of shortfall usually associated with wars and recessions. Just how much can America afford to spend on making life more affordable?

For starters, America’s affordability crisis hardly looks like an emergency. Affordability comes down to purchasing power, which can be boosted by raising real incomes or bringing down prices. When it comes to earnings, Americans are already doing rather well. Real disposable income per person has risen by 13% since the end of 2019. Real median household income reached a record last year. Grocery prices, the cause of much groaning, rose by less than 2% in the year to September. Even petrol is not that dear by historical standards, once you adjust for inflation.

Joe Biden tried to escape a similar political bind when prices and wages both rose after the pandemic by saying, “Look at this chart”, recalls Bharat Ramamurti, who served as an economic adviser to the former president. “It failed spectacularly.” His fellow Democrats are therefore trying to show voters they are listening, and leaving the chart-making to The Economist.

Hakeem Jeffries, the incoming speaker of the House, has laid out a 100-day agenda to unpick some of Mr Trump’s most unpopular handiwork: restoring tax credits that lower health-insurance premiums; reversing cuts to food stamps and Medicaid, the health-care scheme for the poor; and taking aim at tariffs. All would ease the pressure on households. None amounts to an affordability revolution.

Enter Democrats’ grander plans. Some want to expand tax credits for families with children and subsidise child care, others to let younger Americans enroll in Medicare, the public health programme for the old. Price controls are in vogue, including a cap on credit-card interest rates. Mr Trump could veto it all—or not, if he displays the same willingness to work with Democrats as in his first term (or is swayed by anything with his name on it).

The problem is that affordability is not cheap (see chart 1). Reversing Mr Trump’s cuts to Medicaid and food stamps, restoring health-insurance subsidies and ending his tariff extravaganza—which helped bring in $173bn (0.5% of GDP) in customs duties last fiscal year—would add more than $4trn to deficits over the next decade if enacted without offsets, reckons the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, a non-partisan bean-counter. Include the extra interest payments and the bill rises to nearly $5trn, roughly as much as Mr Trump’s giant spending bill from last year is projected to add to the debt.

And that is just the beginning. A proposal by Cory Booker, a Democratic senator, to expand the child tax credit could cost $1.4trn over a decade. Universal child-care subsidies could add $750bn. Lowering Medicare’s eligibility age from 65 to 62 may cost hundreds of billions more.

Democrats are out of the White House, so they can afford to dream. But if those dreams are ever paid for with borrowed money, they could turn into an affordability nightmare. America’s fiscal strain is already pushing up borrowing costs. The “term premium”—the extra return investors demand for lending for longer periods—has risen to around one percentage point, according to a Federal Reserve model. Higher long-term yields, in turn, feed through to mortgages. Yale University’s Budget Lab estimates that deficit-raising legislation since 2015 has added $2,500 a year to payments on a typical new mortgage. This helps explain consumers’ persistent gloom. In 2024 Marijn Bolhuis of the IMF and co-authors found that accounting for borrowing costs explained almost three-quarters of the gap between consumer sentiment and what inflation and unemployment predicted in 2023.

Further borrowing may make matters worse. Research from the Dallas Fed suggests that adding ten percentage points to America’s debt-to-GDP ratio could lift long-term Treasury yields by around 0.3 percentage points, based on historical relationships. With deficits already so large, investors may prove even less forgiving. “You could have made a case for not being worried about the Treasury market four or five years ago,” says Ben Harris, a former Treasury chief economist now at the Brookings Institution, a think-tank. “It’s much harder to make that case today.”

The government could spend more while maintaining fiscal prudence by raising taxes. The easiest way to do this is to reverse tax cuts for the wealthy preserved by Mr Trump’s big spending bill. Restoring the top income-tax rate to 39.6%, from 37%, could raise around $420bn over a decade. A rise in the corporate-tax rate from 21% to 26.5%, as Democrats proposed in 2021, plus higher taxes on investment income and large inheritances, could bring the total to nearly $2trn, according to the Yale Budget Lab (see chart 2). That would barely replace the revenue lost from repealing Mr Trump’s ever-expanding tariffs. More ambitious measures, such as scrapping the “qualified business income deduction”, a tax break for business owners, and taxing unrealised capital gains at death, could lift the total to $3.1trn.

Mr Trump and Republicans, reluctant to undo their signature legislative achievement, would bridle at such ideas. More exotic proposals are either peanuts or just nuts. Taxing artificial-intelligence data centres may garner bipartisan support, but a plan by Mark Warner, a Democratic senator, to strip them of generous depreciation allowances would raise merely $30bn over a decade, according to the Tax Foundation, a think-tank. A wealth tax of the sort championed by Elizabeth Warren, a left-wing senator, may bring in far more, but would be a nightmare to administer, easy to dodge and damaging to investment. It would also struggle to win over moderate Democrats, let alone Republicans. Even deeply Democratic California just roundly rejected a billionaire tax.

The Californian experience points to Democrats’ political problem. In America, raising taxes has become poison. Nearly six in ten Americans told a recent Gallup poll that their taxes were too high. Although they are taxed far less than Europeans, Americans pay much more for things like health care and child care. That leaves little spare cash—and no appetite to pay more in tax.

Once the party of higher taxes, Democrats are losing their appetite for deficit reduction. Even though Mr Biden’s Inflation Reduction Act was designed to pay for itself, that vast stimulus package added nearly $2trn to the deficit. Republicans, meanwhile, have repeatedly blown holes in the budget with unfunded tax cuts. Why should Democrats raise taxes to shrink deficits, the argument goes, if Republicans will use the fiscal room to slash tax rates? James Talarico, the incoming senator from Texas, campaigned on a “cost-of-living tax cut for the middle class”.

Faced with an arithmetic problem as stubborn as the donkey that is their party’s spirit animal, Democrats may instead opt for the lowest-hanging barley. A “Medicare for missiles” bargain, for instance, would offer social spending for them, more military spending for Republicans—and little new revenue to pay for either. This may offer Americans some short-term relief at the cost of long-term pain from higher inflation and rising borrowing costs.

Ultimately, soothing cost-of-living grievances requires more than opening the public purse. It means tackling the bottlenecks driving up the cost of housing, health care, child care and energy with looser zoning rules, faster permitting for energy projects, more competition among hospitals and the like. And if Democrats genuinely want a European-style welfare state in which citizens need not cough up for basic services, it is their job to persuade Americans that this deserves handing over a little more dosh to the government.
